Most nights I'm alone with a stack of spectra, coaxing absorption lines into telling me what a star is made of and how fast it's aging. I grew up in Port-au-Prince watching the sky go orange-black over the hills, and I never quite believed astronomy was a job you could have until I was doing it. Now I do it in Paris, in a building where the radiators clank and the coffee is bad, and somehow that distance still surprises me every day.

## What the light gives up

Stellar spectra are stubborn. A single spectral line can hide rotation, magnetic fields, and chemical history all tangled together, and my work is mostly patient untangling: building models, arguing with noisy data, being wrong in instructive ways. I like problems that punish shortcuts.

## The homesickness I can't quite phrase

It isn't the food or the language, though I miss both. It's a way of being loud in a room, of greeting strangers, that I've had to fold up small here. I haven't found the French word for it yet.

## What I'd recognize on sight

Griot and pikliz — I miss that enough to mimic it badly in my own kitchen. Harder to reproduce: a hand on my forearm mid-sentence, laughter that starts in the ribs and doesn't apologize for the room it takes up, talk that spills past a doorway and pulls in whoever's passing. Here it all stays folded into a murmur. I notice, now, who leans in instead of back, whose hands move when the sentence does — the ones who'd have owned a Port-au-Prince kitchen.
