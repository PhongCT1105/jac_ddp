Every stamped form that reaches my hands in İzmir carries a second story underneath the official one. Once I pulled a 1987 renovation permit for a boundary dispute and found a whole life folded inside it — a widow's signature growing steadier across four amendments, a son added as co-applicant halfway down, a final inspection stamp dated the week a neighboring death certificate was filed two drawers over, though nothing in either paper says the two were connected. Eighteen years of this, and I trust paperwork more than most memories I hear aloud.

### The Weight of Ordinary Records

My job title says municipal archivist, which undersells the strangeness of the work. Most days I move between a basement of century-old ledgers and a database half the staff still distrusts. I can date a building from the typeface on its permit, hear a bribe in the gap between an inspection date and an approval date, notice when a family quietly rebuilt after a fire nobody recorded. None of it is dramatic. It adds up to a private map of the city no single resident, however old, actually carries in full.

### Conversations I Keep Wanting

I look for people who tend systems that outlast their builders — engineers maintaining infrastructure they never designed, genealogists, planners inheriting decisions made in rooms they never entered. I want to know how other cities handle memory nobody properly funds, and I would welcome company for the slow, slightly obsessive thinking I mostly do by myself.
