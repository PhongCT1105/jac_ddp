Ask what time a refrigerated truck actually arrives in Lagos and I'll ask which of six answers you want: scheduled, promised, radioed, actual, or the one I tell the client. Eleven years in cold-chain freight taught me a timetable is a suggestion, and the real craft is building slack nobody notices until it saves a shipment of vaccines or fish.

I coordinate temperature-sensitive freight for distributors and clinics, negotiating between units that lose charge in traffic, warehouses that close early, and drivers who know routes no map shows. Most days are phone calls, generator diesel, and reading a driver's silence before it becomes a problem.

What I radio back to new dispatchers:

- Redundancy beats optimization; the fastest route on paper is worthless the day it floods, so I always carry a second and third plan nobody asked for.
- Trust gets built in the moments things go wrong, so I remember who showed up during the fuel scarcity weeks.
- A dashboard's numbers and a driver's voice often disagree, and the driver is usually closer to true.
- Cold chains fail quietly, so I've trained myself to distrust silence and check in even when nothing seems wrong.

Lately I wonder whether the improvisation here could be studied and taught, not just tolerated. I need someone who has turned tacit routing sense into training that survives a driver quitting — mine dies with whoever holds it, and I haven't found a way to write it down before the next flood proves the rulebook wrong.
