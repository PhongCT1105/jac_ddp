Every Tuesday before dawn I drive up to the weigh station outside our village, where families bring sacks of alpaca fiber sheared by hand. For as long as anyone remembers, buyers from the coast set the price before a sack was even opened, and our families accepted it because refusing meant no sale at all. I am forty-six, and for a dozen years I've negotiated on the cooperative's behalf: pushing back on grading claims, learning to read fiber by micron instead of guessing by feel, and reminding buyers that the herders who raised these animals know exactly what they produce. Some seasons we win a fair price. Other seasons we hold firm and lose a buyer, and I carry that home with me.

## Teaching Myself the Paperwork

What keeps me up most nights now isn't the wool — it's customs codes and bills of lading, which I'm learning from secondhand manuals and a retired exporter in Arequipa who still takes my calls. I want us selling directly to mills abroad instead of through middlemen who each take a cut nobody upstream agreed to. My own uncle sheared alpacas his whole life without ever knowing where the wool ended up.

## What I'm Hoping to Find

I want to talk with anyone who has built an export operation from nothing, understands textile supply chains, or has fought this same negotiation for people who deserved better terms years ago. I'm just as curious about cooperative governance elsewhere — coffee, cacao, anywhere producers organized and it held.
