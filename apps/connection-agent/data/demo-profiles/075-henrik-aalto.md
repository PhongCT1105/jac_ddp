Twenty-two seasons on the same stretch of forest between the river and the fell line, and it still surprises me — a lynx track where there shouldn't be one in April, a burned stand from 2006 finally closing canopy overhead. That's the rhythm I live inside: long solitary days, radio static, snow that keeps its own counsel.

People assume the solitude made me withdrawn. It did the opposite. It made me short on patience for small talk and greedy for the real kind.

Field note, March: an old reindeer fence marked on the survey map no longer matches the ground — thirty years of freeze-thaw shifted a boundary nobody noticed moving. I think about that more than I probably should: how slowly the true shape of a place reveals itself, and how most people want the answer before the walk is even over.

A few things I've learned about myself out here:
- I judge a conversation by what changed in it, not by who sounded clever.
- I trust someone faster if they ask about weather or grief before they ask what I do.
- Silence doesn't unsettle me. I've had years of practice not rushing to fill it.

Looking for people working long, slow problems — restoration, archives, translation, anything paid off in years rather than sprints — and for anyone who chose isolation on purpose, for reasons unlike mine.
