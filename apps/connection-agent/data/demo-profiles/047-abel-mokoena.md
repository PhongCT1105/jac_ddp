The inverter never lies to you. The family arguing over whose fridge gets priority during an outage will lie all afternoon. I've spent twelve years wiring panels and battery banks around Soweto, Diepsloot, and the settlements past Vanderbijlpark, for households and clinics the main grid reaches only when it feels like it. The technical side came fast — I can trace a fault by the smell of hot insulation before opening the panel — but nobody warned me a microgrid is really a small economy in someone's yard, and economies need referees.

What I've come to believe, mostly through bruising arguments:

- A system sized perfectly on paper still fails if the household with the biggest freezer decides the rules don't apply to them at midnight.
- The neighbour who explains the meter in their own words keeps the lights on longer than any warranty I could offer.
- Battery degradation is honest and predictable; patience during the rainy season is neither.
- Clinics teach you the most, because a blackout there isn't an inconvenience, it's a vaccine fridge losing its cold chain by morning.

Now I'm working on what nobody trained me for: fair metering for households sharing one battery bank, instructions a teenager can follow when I'm hours away. Weekends I strip old car batteries for parts and keep half-finishing a solar oven. The metering keeps failing one way: whoever shouts loudest at midnight wins, and the rule bends to them over the fridge that needs it. I'm out of ideas there.
