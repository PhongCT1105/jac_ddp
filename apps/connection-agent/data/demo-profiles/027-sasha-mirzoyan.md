The stamp game took four months to make, and as far as I know nobody has ever finished it in one sitting — you're meant to leave the queue, come back later having half-forgotten your number, then leave again. That's the whole design document: five index cards, a rubber stamp I bought secondhand, and a rule that the office closes for lunch exactly when you're closest to the front.

## What Keeps Getting Made
Most of what I build fits in a coat pocket: paper forms needing three signatures from people who no longer work there, a board game about an inherited apartment nobody can agree how to divide, a phone game where you write a eulogy under a character limit that keeps shrinking as you play. None of it sells much. The ones that make someone laugh and then go quiet are the ones I keep making.

## Wages Elsewhere
By day I do localization QA for a studio with nothing to do with any of this, catching text that overflows its box in Armenian when it fit fine in English. Exacting, unglamorous, and it means I've never had to make a game because it needed to sell. Whether I'd trade that constraint for real money is still an open question.

## Who I'm Listening For
People who've turned paperwork, waiting rooms, or other people's grief into something playable, or who find that a strange thing to want. Board game printers who work in short runs. Anyone building something about inheritance, bureaucracy, or the Armenian diaspora who wants a second set of hands on a rulebook.
