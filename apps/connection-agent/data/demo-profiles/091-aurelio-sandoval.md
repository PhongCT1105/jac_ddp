I have spent my life reading a piece of ground in Mendoza: a malbec leaf that curls in at the edges a full day before anything else on the vine shows strain, a bud that swells a week ahead of where the old calendar says it should, a bunch that loses its dull bloom and turns glassy two days before it wants to come off the vine. Small changes in color, a certain droop before dawn, the way dust smells different two days before hail. That skill is still mine. What I am no longer sure of is whether the ground is still saying what it used to say. I keep the same notebook habits my grandfather kept, and half of what fills the pages now is exception rather than pattern.

Some markers from the years I count by, not by the calendar:

**The year of the late frost that shouldn't have happened.** I was still young enough to think frost had a schedule. It did not come when the old almanac said it would, and we lost more of a block than I want to admit. It taught me that inherited knowledge is a starting draft, not an answer key.

**The season I started keeping two notebooks instead of one.** One for what my hands and eyes told me — the traditional reading. One for numbers: soil moisture, degree days, the dates things actually happened rather than when they were supposed to. I wanted to see where the two disagreed. They disagree more every year.

**The harvest we brought in three weeks early.** Everyone said it was too soon. It wasn't — the heat had moved the whole calendar forward and nobody had told the calendar. That was the season I stopped arguing with the vines about what month it was.

**The winter I spent mostly asking questions instead of giving answers.** I sat with an irrigation engineer, a soil scientist, and an old rival winemaker from the next valley over, and for the first time in years I was the one taking notes. It was uncomfortable. It was also the most useful season I've had in a decade.

**The year I planted something my father would not have planted.** A variety suited to drier, hotter conditions than we used to have, on a slope we used to consider marginal. It is thriving. I think about what else I am wrong about, still.

I am not interested in nostalgia for a climate that is not coming back, and I am not interested in despair either — both feel like ways of avoiding the actual work of figuring out what to do next with the ground in front of me. What I want now are conversations with people watching a system they know well stop behaving the way it used to: farmers elsewhere, water engineers, people who study soil microbes, even someone running a business whose supply chain no longer follows the old rules. I want to trade notebooks, in a manner of speaking. I am also, quietly, trying to write down what I know before I decide it's obsolete — partly for whoever tends this land after me, partly because writing it down is the only way I can tell which parts are still true.
