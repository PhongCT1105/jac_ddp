The horn case goes in the trunk around ten past one, brass still warm, and the alarm goes off again at six so I can be at school before the buses pull in. That gap is where I've lived for most of my twenties. I've stopped pretending it's sustainable. It isn't. I do it anyway, and most weeks it's the best problem I have.

I grew up a couple of streets from a corner where a brass band rehearsed most Sunday afternoons, and I'd sit on the curb, not trying to name what the trumpet was doing, just feeling my chest answer it. My mother found me a student-line horn at a pawn shop when I was ten because I wouldn't stop asking. It had a stuck third valve for almost two years before anybody thought to get it looked at. I learned to work around it, which turned out to be decent training for a lot of things that came later.

For a while after school I thought I'd tour. I sat in wherever they'd let me, drove out for sideman work that barely covered gas, told myself the teaching job was temporary. Then the band director I was subbing for got sick mid-semester, and I ended up in front of sixth graders for six weeks straight. I expected to hate it. Instead I found watching a kid get a clean, ugly, honest B-flat for the first time sits right next to the feeling of a good night at the club, close enough that I've never managed to rank them. I stayed. Some months I still don't fully trust that decision. I haven't undone it either.

The two lives argue with each other more than people assume. Teaching wants patience, a room where nothing surprises anyone for its own sake. Gigging wants the opposite — wants you hunting the surprising thing, a little reckless with the changes. I've caught myself counting off a combo at classroom tempo, and short with a twelve-year-old for not finding the pocket immediately, forgetting it took me years and a stuck valve to get there myself.

Most nights end close to two, the room down to the regulars who never leave before last call, somebody finally calling for the slow one nobody played all set. I count it off soft, just enough for the drummer to catch it, and for four minutes the room breathes at the same tempo I do. Six hours later the classroom smells like pencil shavings instead of spilled beer, and I'm on one knee by a nine-year-old's music stand, walking her fingers back to the note she's been missing all week. Fourth try, she gets it, and looks up like she just caught something falling out of the air.

I'll also talk shop happily: mouthpiece choices, arranging tunes down to three usable notes for a beginner, why certain corners of this city keep a horn audible at every hour. I read more than my schedule suggests I should, mostly biographies of people who kept odd hours for good reasons. I'm good, I think, at noticing when someone's about to quit something they shouldn't — I've been on that ledge myself.
