The first thing I do in any rehearsal room is work out where the kettle goes, because if people aren't comfortable enough to make tea for a stranger, they won't be brave enough to fall over pretending to be someone else. I've directed community casts in halls across Manchester for over two decades — a Methodist hall near Whalley Range, a scout hut in Levenshulme — mostly people with day jobs in accounting or nursing who arrive on a Tuesday and, by December, are unrecognisable to themselves.

### The Kettle Matters More Than the Blocking

I trained formally once, then abandoned most of it. What stayed was an instinct that a rehearsal hall is a social experiment before it's an artistic one — who sits near whom, who's asked to fetch chairs, who apologises before speaking. I spend the first fortnight of any process watching that settle before I touch a script. People think this wastes time. I think it's the only honest way to get a sixty-year-old and a seventeen-year-old trusting each other with difficult material.

### What I'm Actually After Now

My last few shows were devised from what the cast brought in — a retired bus driver's stories, someone's mother's dementia. I'd like company from directors or facilitators solving the same problem elsewhere — village halls, day centres, prisons — building an ensemble from whoever turns up, and from anyone willing to argue over whether calling rehearsal a form of care work lets weak theatre off the hook.
