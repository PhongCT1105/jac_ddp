I spend a lot of time explaining why an eighteen-dollar bag of coffee didn't necessarily make anyone rich at the farm gate, and why a twelve-dollar bag sometimes did. The margins hide in places nobody photographs — drying beds, export permits, the exchange rate on the day a wire clears. I import green coffee from a few regions in Ethiopia, including a cooperative my aunt helped found outside Yirgacheffe, and I've built my work around one refusal: I won't sell the story instead of the coffee. That means turning down deals built around a single photo of a farmer holding cherries, and sitting through contract clauses that are boring and technical, because that's where the leverage actually lives.

Where the actual leverage sits, once you strip away the bag copy:

- Traceability is a spectrum, not a badge; most of what gets marketed as "direct trade" is closer to a well-managed relay than a straight line from farm to roaster.
- A cooperative's internal politics can ruin a harvest faster than bad weather.
- Roasters who push back on pricing turn out to be better long-term partners than the ones chasing a good origin story.
- Currency risk is invisible to the drinker and enormous to everyone upstream of them.

I'm trying to design profit-sharing terms that survive a change in cooperative leadership, and I'd like to compare notes with anyone who has wrestled with a similar distance between labor and pricing power — cocoa, textiles, lithium, any commodity works.
