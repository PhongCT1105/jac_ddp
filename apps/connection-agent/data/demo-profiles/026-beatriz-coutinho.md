The best hour of my day happens underwater, before the sun clears the reef line, when the water off Recife is still cool enough to keep the fish honest and the boats haven't started churning silt. Seven years now grafting coral fragments onto frames along this coast, watching which colonies shrug off the heat and which ones give up in a single bad week.

## Reading the Reef in the Dark
By five I'm tagging fragments by torchlight, logging growth in a waterproof notebook that's outlived three replacements. The site is small enough that I know individual colonies the way a farmer knows fields — this one recovered after 2019, that one hasn't grown a centimeter since. Lately I'm questioning whether the fast growers we keep propagating will actually hold up over a decade, or just look good in a report.

## Conversations I Used to Get Wrong
For years I treated the fishermen working these waters as an obstacle to route around rather than people who'd read this reef longer than any survey line I've run. That's shifting, slowly, over shared coffee and arguments about where the grouper went. Last year a fisherman named Edvaldo showed me the frames I'd sited across his net line were fouling his gear every trip; once we shifted them a boat-length over, his nets ran clean and the coral stopped getting yanked loose. That exchange taught me more about siting restoration than any survey I've logged, and I want more conversations like it — people who know this water well enough to correct me.
