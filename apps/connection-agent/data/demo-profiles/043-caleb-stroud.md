There's a stretch of about ninety seconds, right after someone realizes their friend isn't getting up, where a person's whole operating system reveals itself. Some go quiet and useful. Some narrate. Some run. I've spent eight seasons on search and rescue above nine thousand feet in Colorado, and that window is the part of the job I keep turning over, long after the report is filed and the patient is off the mountain.

Winters I go still. I read philosophy of mind and a fair amount of stoic and existentialist writing, not to master it, but because it takes seriously how much of what we call character shows up under load. I cook slowly, split wood, answer almost nothing on a schedule.

A partial inventory, compiled above nine thousand feet and revised each winter by the woodstove:

- Competence under pressure is mostly rehearsal wearing a costume of instinct; the calm ones practiced somewhere first.
- Fear and focus share a nervous system, and the difference often comes down to what you already decided about your own hands.
- The scariest calls rarely have the most blood; they're the ones where nobody agrees yet what's happening.
- People remember who stayed, not who was right.

The person I most want across the table is an ER charge nurse — the kind who can tell you, precisely, the shift when her hands stopped shaking, because that means she's done the accounting on her own nervous system instead of just surviving it. Also happy to argue Camus with someone who'll push back.
