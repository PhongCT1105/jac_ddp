## Wood remembers what actors forget

I spend most days in a workshop that smells of lime shavings and beeswax, threading string through the shoulders of characters who haven't been born yet. For over two decades I've carved marionettes for a small touring company that plays village halls, school gymnasiums, sometimes courtyards where the wind decides how a puppet moves. I joint the limbs so grief and mischief can travel through the same hinge. Half of the work is invention, half is closer to embalming — I finish a figure already knowing how it will eventually fail, and I build that failure in gently, so a snapped string reads as fate rather than accident.

## What I keep circling back to

Puppets outlast the productions they were made for. I still keep a fox from a show that closed before some of the current actors were born, and I mend her jaw every few seasons because someone still wants to watch her speak. That stubborn continuity interests me more than novelty ever has. I find myself asking other trades how they handle objects meant to be used until they break — instrument makers, boatwrights, the men who tune church bells.

## Where this could lead

I'd welcome conversation with someone building mechanisms far outside theatre, a writer wanting a puppet's-eye view of a story, or anyone who has spent years getting quietly better at one stubborn craft.
