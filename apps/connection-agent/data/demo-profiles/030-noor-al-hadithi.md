A father once asked me to explain a diagnosis "the way you'd tell a five-year-old, because I can't hear it any other way right now." That sentence rearranged how I practice. Twelve years into pediatric care in Amman, most of my real skill has nothing to do with charts or dosing and everything to do with reading a terrified parent's face and finding the one sentence that lets them breathe again.

### The Fear Under the Question
Parents rarely ask what they actually want to know. "Is this normal?" usually means "did I do something wrong," and I've learned to answer the second question first. I collect these translations the way some people collect recipes, and I test new ones on my own family before I trust them with a stranger's worst week.

### A Book With No Diagnoses In It
I am drafting a picture book that teaches nothing medical at all. It's about the feeling of not understanding a grown-up's words and being scared anyway, meant to sit in waiting rooms and hand to a sibling while their brother is behind a curtain. I want it tested by real clinics, not just praised by people who like the idea of it.

I'm looking for illustrators who work in warm, uncluttered lines, writers who've simplified something frightening for children, and clinicians anywhere who've built their own way of saying hard things gently.
