Ten years ago I withdrew from a competition three weeks before the deadline, because I'd spent a weekend inside the building we were meant to demolish and couldn't stop thinking about the brick coursing in the stairwell. That was the last new building I ever tried to design. Since then I've worked only on structures that already exist: a printing works from 1911 turned into studios, a fjord-side warehouse now housing a swimming club, a concrete stairwell block from the seventies that everyone wanted torn down and that I fought to keep.

## Working with what refuses to be erased

People assume retrofitting means compromise, but I've found the opposite. A building with eighty years of settling, rewiring, and bad additions already carries an argument inside it, and my job is to listen for that argument rather than impose a new one. The limits sharpen the thinking. I can now read a structure's history in its floor levels, its window proportions, the faint lines where a wall used to stand.

## Oslo from underneath

I know this city less by its skyline than by what's under the render: which basements flood, which 1930s stairwells have the best light, which archive clerk actually answers the phone. What I still can't answer myself is how you tell, before you open a wall, whether a building wants to be kept or only wants you to think so. Every method I've tried breaks down on the ones that surprise me, and I suspect whoever actually has an answer isn't an architect at all.
