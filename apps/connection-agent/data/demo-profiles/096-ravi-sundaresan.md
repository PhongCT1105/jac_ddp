Forty years I spent making certain two trains never wanted the same stretch of track at once — starting as a boy tracing cable routes near Coimbatore, finishing as the engineer the control room rang at two in the morning when a relay chattered in a way nobody trusted enough to ignore. I retired eighteen months ago with a pension, a knee that predicts rain better than the newspaper, and a suspicion that most of what I know precisely I will never need again. So I have taken up things I have no use for on purpose — a violin I cannot tune without wincing, a shelf of Tamil grammar I read like scripture, a chess set where I lose to myself most Tuesdays and call it fair. My wife, Meena, finds this funny. I find it necessary.

Interlocking logic teaches you to distrust convenience and love constraint, and that habit outlived the job. Here is the ledger I still keep, mostly for my own peace — what four decades left me holding, and what still refuses to resolve now that the emergencies have stopped ringing.

**What the block sections settled for good**

- A system is only as honest as its worst-case assumption. I still test every claim — a doctor's, a newspaper's, my own — by asking what it assumes will go wrong.
- Redundancy is not timidity. Two independent ways to catch the same danger is honesty about how much any single method deserves.
- The safest default is to stop, not hope. Every circuit I signed off failed toward red, never green — an instinct that now follows me into arguments I dislike.

That certainty was comfortable, and I miss it more than expected. What I do now is stand in front of things with no fail-safe state and no correct answer, on purpose, because I think I owe my mind that discomfort.

**What refuses to close out**

- Carnatic music: my sister sings ragas I can name but cannot yet hear apart, and I want someone patient enough to explain what a trained ear does that mine does not.
- Watercolor: I have ruined eleven sheets of good paper painting the Noyyal river at dusk, because pigment will not obey a schematic and I keep expecting it to.
- Beekeeping: my neighbor keeps two hives and swears there is logic to how they choose a new queen. I suspect he is right, and it irritates me how much I want to understand it.
- Whether I am the only one: forty years I kept trains from wanting the same stretch of track, and I have not worked out if every retired keeper of a zero-tolerance system ends up, like I have, learning a violin and a grammar and a losing chess game just to have something in his evenings with no fail-safe state — or if that is a peculiarity of one Coimbatore man with too much unscheduled time.
