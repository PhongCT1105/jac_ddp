The first thing an athlete tells me is rarely where it hurts. A striker at my club calls a torn hamstring "stiffness" and asks when he can train again. A runner calls a stress fracture "a niggle" until she can't walk to the metro. Eleven years on treatment tables — a hospital ward first, now a semi-professional club here in Marseille and a crew of amateur runners along the Corniche — taught me that pain talk is its own language, rarely translating cleanly into tissue damage.

What holds me now isn't the anatomy, which I know cold, but the gap between what people say and what's underneath. A few things I keep circling back to:

- The men on the squad minimize until the body forces the issue, while the runners I see, several training for a first marathon, over-describe every twinge because nobody taught them what normal effort feels like.
- The most reliable athletes aren't the toughest talkers, they're the ones who locate pain precisely and rate it the same way twice.
- Fear rewrites vocabulary before it touches function: one bad ACL and a player calls any unrelated knock on that leg "my knee thing" for years.
- Working across languages flattens pain reports, so I ask the same question three different ways before trusting an answer.

I keep notes now on the exact words people use before a diagnosis and after one, whether the vocabulary changes once pain has a name. I ask patients this and can't stop asking it elsewhere: when it hurts, how do you decide what to tell me?
