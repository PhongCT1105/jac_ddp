The back room of my shop smells like lanolin, dust, and wool folded the same way for forty years. I go in before opening, before the fluorescent lights hum on out front, and run my hand across a stack of Heriz that came in last month. You can tell the decade a rug was tied by how the wool takes light — not something anyone taught me in words, something my fingers learned at my father's elbow in a shop half this size, six thousand miles from here.

I was twenty-two when I left, and I won't pretend I understood at the time how permanent it would turn out to be — I thought I was going for two or three years, to open a second branch of a business that no longer exists in the form I left it. I arrived with eleven kilos of luggage and a notebook of my father's supplier contacts, half already out of date. Los Angeles had a rug district then that doesn't exist anymore either, a few blocks where every third storefront smelled like mine does now. I learned English from customers who wanted a story with their purchase, and learned fast the story mattered more to them than the knot count, so I made sure to have both.

The historian in me started by accident, with a runner bought sight unseen from an estate sale — mediocre condition, but it carried a repair pattern I recognized from a village outside Tabriz, a specific way of re-tying a damaged section I'd only ever seen my grandfather do. I spent four months tracking the family workshop that used it, corresponding with a cousin of a cousin still living near there, and finished with more notes on that one piece than I had receipts. Now I keep a cabinet of these dossiers — not appraisals, just my own record of where a piece was born and what happened since to the town that made it. Most customers don't ask. I write them anyway.

I'm not sentimental about the merchandise itself. I haggle hard and have walked from pieces I loved because the seller wanted romance-money instead of market price. But the towns get me quietly. Some of the villages whose looms fill my inventory have half the population they had when I was young; a few have emptied into cities, the weaving passed to nobody. I've gone back three times, always telling myself it's for sourcing, always coming home with more notebook pages than rugs.

What I want here isn't customers — the shop runs fine without new friends through the door. I want people curious about the years between a sheep and a finished piece: dye chemists, conservators, anyone doing oral history with tradespeople who left home young, students who've read more than they've touched. I like being corrected, told my favorite attribution is probably wrong, because half the time it is. And I've become, against my own expectations, decent company for people working out the arithmetic of a second self built somewhere far from the first — not advice, just company for a loneliness that shows up on ordinary Tuesdays, not the anniversaries you'd expect. Come argue with me about whether a border changes what a pattern means. I keep tea too strong and hours later than I should.
