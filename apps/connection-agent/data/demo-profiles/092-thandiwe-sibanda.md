Dear stranger,

I am writing this at eleven at night, the only hour the phone stops ringing and the only one I trust myself to say what I mean instead of what sounds tidy. Forgive me if this reads less like an introduction and more like a letter, because that is what it is. I wanted one person out there who has never met me to know roughly where I stand.

I am a lawyer in Harare, forty-one. Most of my working life has gone into cases that pay little or nothing — land and inheritance disputes, usually involving widows and younger siblings told, sometimes gently and sometimes not, that the house or the field was never really going to be theirs. The law here is often less unkind than custom is. A great deal of my work is the unglamorous business of proving that on paper, in a register, in a queue, to a clerk who has seen four hundred cases like this one and is tired too. I have learned to be patient in rooms designed to exhaust patience, and that winning a case and fixing a life are two different achievements — I have only ever been qualified for the first.

I should tell you plainly: I am tired. Not in a way that makes a good story — tired the way a well runs low after a long dry season, still giving water, but you can hear the difference in the bucket. I am not finished, though. I want to be honest about both things at once, because I think most interesting people carry both, and pretending otherwise makes for worse conversation.

If you want somewhere to start with me: I am curious how other places handle proof when the paper trail is thin or was never made, because that is the real bottleneck in my cases, not the law itself. I am curious about people doing unglamorous, slow-moving work in other fields, whatever well they draw from and how they keep it from running dry. I am curious about grief in a professional register, how others who sit across from loss for a living carry it home. I read more than I should about land tenure history, for reasons that started as work and became genuine interest.

I am not looking for anyone to fix me, and I am wary of anyone offering to. What I would like is correspondence with people whose work has taught them something true about endurance or fairness — other lawyers doing thankless work elsewhere, mediators, people in medicine or social work who know that particular fatigue from the inside, historians of land who might sharpen my thinking. Or simply someone who asks good questions and does not need me to be inspiring in order to keep talking to me.

If any of that sounds like you, I would be glad to hear from it. I promise to answer, even if it takes me a while — most things in my life do.

With more hope than I let on,
Thandiwe
