Water finds the lowest point in an argument the way it finds the lowest point in a street — learned that twice, once in a monsoon and once in a ward meeting that ran four hours over.

Nineteen years moving between two rooms. In one: contour maps, canal cross-sections, the arithmetic of where a backwater goes when it has nowhere else to go. In the other: plastic chairs, a councillor's ceiling fan, someone explaining why the drain outside their shop can't be touched this year, maybe not next either. Both rooms teach the same lesson from different sides — a flood plan that exists only on paper is a rumor, not a plan.

What that means most weeks:
- walking canal stretches at low tide with a notebook, since satellite images miss what people quietly build into the banks
- sitting through meetings I have no vote in, since that's where a plan survives contact with real life or doesn't
- translating hydrology into sentences a shopkeeper can argue with me about

I care less for the elegant masterplan than the patched-together version fifteen stakeholders can each still recognize as partly theirs. Kochi doesn't get a blank contour map to start over on.

Unsettled question I keep circling: how much of resilience is engineering, how much is trust, rebuilt one negotiated culvert at a time. Glad to talk with anyone who's sat where infrastructure and politics stop being separate subjects.
