I once spent three weeks rebuilding a municipal budget spreadsheet from Copenhagen's own open-data portal, only to find the number everyone quoted in council meetings didn't match any line item in the actual ledger. That gap between the figure people repeat and the figure that's true is basically my beat now. I cover housing costs and municipal budgets, which mostly means reading procurement documents no one wants to read and asking finance officers the same question three ways until the answer stops slipping.

What unsettles me is how easily a chart can make an argument feel finished. A rising line looks like proof even when the axis is doing the work, so I try to treat every graphic I publish as something to interrogate rather than admire. The interrogation list currently includes:

- Whether a "before and after" housing chart shows a real policy effect or just a change in who got counted.
- How to write about rent increases without letting one dramatic number stand in for a whole city's experience.
- Where clarifying data ends and performing certainty I don't actually have begins.
- What it would take to make a budget document as readable as the headline written about it.

Outside deadlines I read about the history of statistics and argue with anyone willing about visual rhetoric. My working theory: any chart persuasive enough to change a policy vote is probably hiding more than it shows. Nobody's talked me out of it yet — I'm still waiting for the argument that will.
