Vanilla season, early flowering — I keep these notes the way my father kept his, in whatever notebook is closest.

Flowering week, rain late again. Eleven days late this year and the upper-valley growers are already arguing about it, as if I control the sky. Walked three plots with old Rakoto, who still hand-pollinates every flower himself at seventy-one and refuses help. Same joke every time. I laugh every time. This is also the job.

Mid-season, buyer call. A broker in the capital wanted a price locked before the curing results were even in. I told him no, the way I always tell them no — figures on green vanilla mean nothing until we know the cure ratio, and I've watched cooperatives get squeezed by optimism dressed up as urgency. My notebook from four seasons back still has the numbers from the year we sold too early. Still stings to reread. I keep that page on purpose.

Late season, family trouble. Two households nearly came to blows over a boundary hedge that has moved, by everyone's account, forty centimeters over ten years. Spent an afternoon between them with tea going cold, listening more than talking — most of what mediation actually is. Nobody thanked me. By evening they were repairing the hedge together, which is the only thanks I need.

Curing house, night shift. Checked moisture by feel and smell, the way I was taught before anyone owned a proper hygrometer. I bought one three years ago and still trust my hands first, the dial second. Wrote pages of weather notes — humidity, wind direction, the smell the air gets before a cyclone swings wide of us instead of through. Eleven years of these now. Someone, someday, is going to want them, even if it's only me.

I run a small growers' cooperative on the northeastern coast, which mostly means I'm a translator between people who grow something delicate and people who buy it without ever having smelled a flowering vine. I negotiate contracts, settle disputes that have nothing to do with vanilla and everything to do with vanilla, and write down every price, storm, and broken promise, because memory is unreliable and paper is not. I came into this by accident — my mother fell ill, someone needed to keep the ledger, and I never handed it back.

Running list, the one I don't show anyone. Pricing is still reactive — we wait for the broker's number and argue about it after, when we should be setting terms before the flowers even open. Someone who has actually rebuilt a cooperative's pricing from scratch, not just written a paper about it, could save us a decade of guessing. The record problem is worse than the price problem, if I'm honest: eleven years of notebooks in my own shorthand, and no plan for what happens to them if I'm hit by a truck on the coast road. Whoever has already solved that for coffee or cacao has probably solved it for vanilla too, they just don't know it yet. Note to self, not the notebook: if either kind of person ever passes through the valley, don't let the conversation stop at the price of a kilo. And once, just once, find someone who also ended up running a place they never applied to run, and ask how they knew it was still theirs to leave.
