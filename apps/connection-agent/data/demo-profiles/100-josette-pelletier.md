I keep a running list of words I refuse to let flatten into their nearest translation. Here is a small selection, offered the way I'd hand someone a jar of things I've collected on walks.

**dépaysement.** French has this word for the mild vertigo of being somewhere unfamiliar, and English just doesn't. I spent a year of my twenties translating tourism brochures that rendered it as "a sense of adventure," which isn't it at all — closer to homesickness in reverse. I think about this word whenever a client asks me to make a sentence "say the same thing" in the other language. Sometimes the honest answer is that it can't, and the job is to build a bridge that holds weight rather than a perfect mirror.

**franglais.** The register I grew up switching into at family dinners in Winnipeg without anyone deciding to. I used to be embarrassed by it, as if it proved I didn't fully own either language. Now I teach a Tuesday evening class where half my adult students code-switch the same way, and I tell them it isn't a failure of grammar, it's a third thing with its own logic. Watching someone stop apologizing for how they talk is one of my favorite parts of teaching.

**la langue de ma grand-mère.** I'm careful with this one — it isn't mine to name casually or hold up as a specimen. It was my grandmother's first language, spoken by almost no one left in our family, and I'm learning what I can from the handful of elders and community classes willing to teach an outsider-adjacent adult. Progress is slow, and I've made peace with the fact that I'll likely never be fluent. What I'm really after is smaller and more honest: knowing enough that if my grandmother were still here, some of what she carried wouldn't have needed translating between us.

**relève.** The next generation coming up to take over. My best students eventually correct my grammar, and I've decided that's the actual goal, not a threat to my authority. I want the same for the language my grandmother spoke — not me as its custodian, but younger cousins with better ears than mine picking it up before it survives only in recordings.

**tricoter.** To knit — how I actually work: two threads, French and English, crossed and looped until the pattern holds on both sides. Legal documents, a small press's short story collection, a settlement agency's intake forms — different yarns, same motion.

**vis-à-vis.** Literally, face-to-face-with — not a mirror, not a translation, the person a sentence is actually aimed at. English keeps filing it under "regarding," which throws out the whole point: a vis-à-vis faces you, doesn't stand behind you or beside you. I keep one seat at every table turned a few degrees toward the door, angled for whoever walks in already good at this kind of noticing — someone who'll hear a word I've been mistranslating for years and hand it back to me the right shape.
