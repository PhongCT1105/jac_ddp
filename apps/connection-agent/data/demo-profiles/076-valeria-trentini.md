Three chords into a rehearsal I already know if the soprano slept. That's most of the job: not the stage, the room next door to it, an upright with two dead keys, and me holding an entire orchestra in two hands while keeping my opinions about the conductor's tempo to myself.

Milan, thirty-six, coaching singers since I was twenty-two. I know something like a hundred roles well enough to sing them cold — Tosca's arc bar by bar, where Amina's phrasing wants to breathe, the exact beat the Marschallin stops being amused. I have sung exactly none of them for an audience. People assume that's a failed ambition. It isn't. I chose the seat, not the spotlight, and I'd rather know the whole score than just my line in it.

What the work is, most days:
- correcting a diphthong at 9am, correcting a marriage's worth of nerves by noon
- learning enough Russian to catch a wrong stress before the coach does
- watching someone find a note they'd given up on, from four feet away, saying nothing

(Tuesday note to self: stop finishing the tenors' sentences for them.)

Lately I'm curious about other people who do the load-bearing half of someone else's performance and stay unnamed in the program — second chairs, surgical assistants, the editor before the byline. I'd like to compare what that does to an ego over twenty years, and whether you ever wanted the credit, or just wanted the room quiet enough to hear what's actually wrong.
