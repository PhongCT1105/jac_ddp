I spent fourteen years treating cancer before I understood that curing it and caring for the person having it were sometimes two different jobs, and I wasn't always doing the second one well. At forty-two I moved from oncology to palliative care in Kraków, which surprised colleagues who read it as retreat. It wasn't. It was the harder conversation, chosen on purpose.

Most days now involve pain control, breathlessness, the logistics of dying at home versus on a ward, and sitting with families while they rearrange what they thought next month would look like. Outside the clinic I garden to the point of compulsion - a small plot with too many tomato varieties, a compost system rebuilt four times, a running dispute with a neighbor about pruning. Same instinct as the work: attend closely, accept what you cannot force, keep a plan anyway.

Between rounds and the compost heap, this is what's held up:

- The best clinical teams I've worked with treated silence as information, not a gap to fill.
- Gardening taught me more about tolerating uncertain outcomes than residency ever did.
- I distrust any framework for grief that promises stages instead of weather.
- The hardest skill in medicine isn't diagnosis, it's pacing the truth to what someone can actually absorb that day.

What I want is conversation that sits in a long silence without rushing to fill it, lets a change of subject stand as its own answer, and doesn't need to end somewhere tidy. Slow is fine. Unresolved is fine too.
