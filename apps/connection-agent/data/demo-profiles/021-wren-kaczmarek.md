I count Chicago's trees for a living, block by block, and I still know exactly how many stood on the block I grew up on: none. That absence shaped how I read a city — not as skyline or parks but as canopy, or the want of it, and who gets to stand in shade come August.

## What the canopy map actually shows

My days split between GIS layers and sidewalks. I built a tree inventory for a stretch of the south side that no agency had ever fully surveyed, cross-checking species, trunk diameter, and crown health against old redlining maps. The overlap is not subtle. I use that data to push for planting budgets, but the map only opens the door — the real work is block club meetings, asking people what they want growing outside their own windows before a single sapling goes in.

## Standing under something instead of arguing about it

I trust a caliper tape over a slogan, though I've stopped separating the two. I read utility easement disputes and old aldermanic planting ledgers the way some people read novels, hunting for the moment a block's shade got traded away. I want to meet people working on heat mapping, housing policy, or organizing who've tried to make a bureaucracy plant something it never budgeted for — or just anyone who wants to argue about which species survive road salt.
