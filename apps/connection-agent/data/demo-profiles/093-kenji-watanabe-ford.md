People ask me things at markets often enough that I keep a running list. Here's the honest version.

**How long does one chair actually take you?**
Three weeks to four months, depending on how much the wood argues with me. A dining chair with a woven seat moves fast once the joinery is settled. A rocking chair, where I carve the runners to match one person's stride, can eat a season. I build one at a time, start to finish — I tried two in parallel my first year and hated both by the end.

**What did you do before this?**
I managed a structural engineering team in Vancouver, mostly seismic retrofits on older buildings. Good pay, work that mattered. I left at thirty-one, tired of describing joints and load paths in meetings instead of ever touching the materials. Six months' notice, which surprised everyone, including me. I don't miss the calendar — I used to schedule life in fifteen-minute blocks, and now a Tuesday can be entirely about one mortise that isn't seating right.

**Isn't this just a hobby that got out of hand?**
I keep books, I pay a lease on a shop in East Van, and a chair has to sell for what it needs to sell for if I want to still be doing this in ten years. The romance wears off around year two. What's left is work I still want more than the title at the engineering firm.

**What wood won't you touch?**
I've stopped using wenge — the dust isn't worth what it does to my lungs. I'm drawn instead to reclaimed Douglas fir from old mill buildings, where the grain reads like a ledger: fire years, drought years, a nail from 1958 that ruins a blade and tells you something true about the building it came from.

**What's a question nobody asks that you wish they would?**
What I got wrong on my first commission. I under-built a chair back for a heavier client and it failed within a year. I redid it for free, and I've overbuilt slightly ever since, on purpose — a little less elegance for a lot more sleep. Nobody asks either whether I build things I don't sell. I do, once a year, no client, no deadline, just to remember why the rest of it is worth the trouble.

**What are you trying to get better at right now?**
Steam bending, which I can do but not with the ease of someone who's broken enough pieces to stop fearing the wood. I'm also circling the idea of teaching a small class, though I'm not sure I trust myself yet to explain what I mostly do by feel.

**Who do you want to end up talking to here?**
People who left something secure for something slower and can talk honestly about the year it got hard, not just the origin story. Structural engineers who think about material behavior the way I now think about grain. Anyone running a small business around a physical craft who's solved pricing without underselling themselves. And anyone willing to argue about whether a chair needs armrests to be a good chair.
