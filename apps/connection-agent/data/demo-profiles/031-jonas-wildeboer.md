### Twenty-two years reading steel taught me what an orchard would later teach me twice as hard

I spent my working life on bridge crews, designing the connections where one beam meets another and a fraction of a degree in fabrication decides whether the thing holds through a Vermont February. When my knees finally gave out on the inspection catwalks, I sold the place near Burlington and bought eleven acres with a barn that leans four degrees off plumb. That felt closer to home than the office ever did. Now I keep thirty hives strung along the treeline and press cider from whatever the trees actually give me, not what a nursery catalog promised.

### The Red Delicious problem, unresolved since 1987

I planted six of them my first year, on some misguided loyalty to what a grocery store told me an apple ought to be. They have never once made a cider worth drinking — mealy, sweet in the wrong direction, dishonest fruit dressed up in a good name. I have not cut them down. I argue with them every October instead, and I suspect I always will.

What I'm after now is someone who can talk hive genetics, soil chemistry, or fatigue cracking with equal seriousness, and who won't flinch when I get worked up about a piece of fruit.
