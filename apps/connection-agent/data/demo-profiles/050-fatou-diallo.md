The cart has four wheels, two of which stick, and on Wednesdays I push it through the sandy lanes near the market so kids can trade a comic for something they'll pretend not to like. The reading room is the same work — a converted garage where anyone under eighteen can sit an hour without an adult asking what they're supposed to be learning from it.

For years I watched donors send crates of "improving" literature nobody opened, while water-stained detective novels got passed hand to hand until the spines gave out. My actual work, I decided, is defending bad taste — the right to read trash, below level, the same book nine times, without correction.

What the cart has taught me:

- A boy who reads three pages of a real book and quits is doing more than one who reads zero pages of an "appropriate" one.
- Circulation counts lie about what matters; the honest measure is whether someone comes back unasked, hunting for the next one.
- Censorship rarely announces itself; it arrives as a well-funded list of what children should be reading instead.
- A cart that breaks down teaches you more about a neighborhood's walking routes than any survey would.

Last month a girl of fifteen planted herself at the cart and asked, flatly, for a book about a woman who leaves her husband and isn't punished for it by the last page. I didn't have one. I spent three weeks phoning bookstalls until a secondhand Senegalese novel turned up, and put it in her hands before I'd finished reading it myself.
