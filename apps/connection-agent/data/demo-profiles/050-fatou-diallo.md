The cart has four wheels, two of which stick, and on Wednesdays I push it through the sandy lanes near the market so kids can trade a comic for a battered copy of something they'll pretend not to like. That's the job as much as the reading room is — a converted garage with mismatched chairs where anyone under eighteen can sit for an hour without an adult asking what they're supposed to be learning from it.

For years I watched donors send crates of "improving" literature nobody opened, while water-stained detective novels got passed hand to hand until the spines gave out. Somewhere in there I decided my actual work is defending bad taste — the right to read trash, to read below your level, to read the same volume nine times, without correction.

What the cart has taught me:

- A boy who reads three pages of a real book and quits is doing something the boy who reads zero pages of an "appropriate" one is not.
- Circulation counts lie about what matters; the honest measure is whether someone comes back unasked, hunting for the next one.
- Censorship rarely announces itself — it usually arrives as a well-funded list of what children ought to be reading instead.
- A cart that breaks down teaches you more about a neighborhood's real walking routes than any survey would.

I want conversations with people building small, stubborn institutions elsewhere — seed libraries, repair cafés — and anyone who has thought hard about minds between eleven and sixteen.
