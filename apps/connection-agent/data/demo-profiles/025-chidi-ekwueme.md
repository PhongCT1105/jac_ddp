The notebook that survives every response is never the line list or the case definition. It's a battered spiral one where I write down the actual sentences people say to me in the field: "Is it true this started with the vaccine team?" "Why did the ambulance skip our street?" Six years into this work, based in Kigali, I've come to think the pathogen is usually the easy part. What spreads faster, and costs more if it's left alone, is a rumor nobody answered within two days.

### What the case counts don't show
My training is in field epidemiology, but most of my working hours go to something closer to translation — sitting with community health workers, market vendors, and radio hosts, turning suspicion into a conversation instead of a directive. I've learned to read a village meeting the way other people read a spreadsheet: where the silence sits, who glances at whom before answering.

### The question ledger
That notebook has hundreds of entries now, sorted loosely by fear rather than by illness. I want to know whether the patterns hold in places I've never worked, so I'm glad to talk with anyone who studies rumor, oral history, or crisis communication, in health or well outside it.

### Where this goes next
I'd like to turn the ledger into something more lasting, a shared method or a stubborn small archive, and I'm looking for collaborators drawn to the texture of how trust actually breaks, not to another dashboard.
