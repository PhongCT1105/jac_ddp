Kneel in pine litter in October, after the first hard frost has broken the top centimeter of soil but before the ground has fully locked shut, and you will catch a particular smell - wet moss, cold stone, something faintly like sherry. I have organized a fair portion of my adult life around getting to be down there with a trowel when it appears. Most people who work near me for long enough learn to recognize it too, whether they meant to or not.

I run a small research group north of Uppsala studying how fungal networks are distributed through boreal forest floor - which species turn up together, how that changes when a stand is thinned, burned, or left alone for eighty years. It is unglamorous work by design: a soil core, a catalogue of what is living in it, months waiting on sequencing, and mostly a lesson in the size of what you do not know yet. In my twenties that ratio frustrated me badly enough that I nearly left the field, during a second year when every hypothesis I tested came back inconclusive and I was certain I had built a career on quicksand. A supervisor told me, not unkindly, that certainty was the wrong thing to chase underground, and I should learn to enjoy the texture of not-knowing or find something else to do. I decided I liked the texture.

The complication is that a documentary crew once filmed one of our field seasons, cut it together with confident narration about trees "talking" to each other through fungal threads, and suddenly strangers were writing to me wanting confirmation that forests have feelings, or memory, or intention. I understand the appeal - I have felt something like awe kneeling over an old root mat myself - but I have become the person at gatherings who has to gently complicate a beautiful idea, a role I never auditioned for. The science is stranger than the myth, and also far less settled than the myth implies, and I would rather sit with that discomfort in public than round it off into something tidier.

People who work seriously with systems that resist clean answers tend to recognize each other quickly, in my experience - something in how carefully we hedge a sentence, maybe. Someone who has had to defend a field against its own popularizers, or a parent whose child asks unanswerable questions about mushrooms at dinner, generally asks better questions than the adults who arrive wanting the documentary version confirmed. I still spend most autumn weekends outdoors, cold-fingered and unhurried, and am easy to draw into shop talk over coffee if you bring a real question rather than a settled opinion.

Here is the one I have not answered after fifteen years of doing this: when two separate fungal colonies turn up sharing the same root system, is that a connected thing behaving as one, or two organisms that each independently favor the same patch of wet, dark ground. The instruments that would settle it plainly do not exist yet, and I have stopped assuming the answer will be tidy either way.
