1. I run a hut at 2,340 meters in the Tyrolean Alps, three hours' walk from the nearest road in good weather. Summers I feed and bed down whoever arrives. Winters it's mostly me, the wind, and a diesel generator I've learned to argue with.

2. Thirty-nine years old. I trained as a structural engineer, then took over the hut from a warden who'd run it for thirty-one years and wanted, in his words, "to sleep somewhere the walls don't move." I understood him fully by my first winter.

3. The generator has taught me more about electrical systems than university ever did - university never charged me a night at minus twenty for getting something wrong. I keep a logbook of every fix, nine years running, the closest thing I have to a research career.

4. I can read an approaching front off the western ridge about forty minutes before the forecast catches up. It isn't intuition, just pattern recognition repeated a few thousand times. I'm curious why some people trust that built instinct and others always reach for the phone first.

5. One October a couple arrived separately, clearly estranged, and ended up sharing the last free table because there was nowhere else to sit. By morning they'd rebooked their descent to leave together.

6. What I'm bad at: leaving. I've missed two funerals because the pass was closed and I judged the walk down too risky. I stand by the calls and still feel their weight.

7. Winter isn't empty. I repair equipment, read slowly, correspond by letter with two people I've never met, and log bird sightings for a research station in Innsbruck - mostly ptarmigan, occasionally a golden eagle over the ridge.

8. I used to think isolation was the price of this work. Now I think it's closer to the point - it gives me useful distance from other people's urgency. Guests arrive mid-crisis constantly, and it's easier to see which parts are real.

9. I ration my own company on purpose - months of constant strangers, then months of almost none. Most people would do better with some small version of that rhythm.

10. What I want next: to turn the bird logs into something an ornithologist would take seriously, and I'm quietly redesigning the hut's water intake for a rebuild in three summers. I'd take real pleasure in an engineer telling me where my drawings are wrong.

I'm hoping to hear from people doing engineering or field research I could actually be useful to, correspondents willing to write real letters instead of messages, anyone who has kept a hut, a lighthouse, or a long watch of any kind, and people willing to talk plainly about what a year built from extremes does to someone over time. I'm slow to trust and quick to feed you, and I'd rather start with something specific and true than small talk that goes nowhere.
