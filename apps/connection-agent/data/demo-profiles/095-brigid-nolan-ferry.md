I have sat at more deathbeds than I can count — not from any calling, but because thirty years ago a hospital chaplain covering nights went out sick and nobody else would take the shift. [Not quite true. I knew what I was agreeing to. I just didn't believe it would ask this much of me, this long.] I still do the work, three days a week now, in a hospice on Dublin's northside, where the mountains out the window get called "grand" by people who won't see another sunset.

People assume the job needs certainty — a settled answer about God, or the absence of one. I came in with an ordinary Catholic formation and leave most shifts these days without much of it intact. [Not gone. Worn down to something usable, the way a bar of soap wears to a sliver that still lathers.] What I have instead is a working belief that attention is itself a kind of mercy, that the dying are usually less afraid of the dark than of dying alone or unremembered, and that you can sit with a devout engineer, a lapsed farmer, and a woman who told me flatly she believed in nothing but compost, and offer each the same thing: someone who won't flinch and won't perform.

The part I don't say out loud, in case it sounds like I've lost the plot, is that death seems to me less a wall than people assume and more of a — [I was going to write "threshold," the chaplain's word, worn smooth from overuse] — a change of state the dying often sense coming from some distance off, calmly, the way you'd feel weather turning before the sky does anything about it. I don't preach this. It sits in me as observation, held loosely enough that a good argument could still move it. [Ask on a bad week and I'll say I've no idea what I'm talking about. Also true.]

I read obituaries the way others read the sports pages, I'm relearning the Irish my grandmother spoke, and I'm taking sailing lessons out of Dun Laoghaire, against my own predictions. [My surname already has a boat in it. I didn't choose the lessons for the joke, but I've stopped pretending the joke isn't there.] I want fewer answers now and better questions — I'd rather spend two hours with someone who studies what the brain does in its last minutes than another polite chat about whether the work is hard. It is. Never the interesting part.

What I'm after is not a grief group or a theology seminar, though I'd take either. I want people doing unglamorous work at the edges of things — palliative doctors, death doulas, anyone who's nursed a parent through the last months, humanist celebrants, clergy willing to argue with me properly, and anyone carrying an odd private cosmology they've never said aloud to a stranger. [A good ghost story will also do.] I'll trade what thirty years at bedsides taught me for whatever you've learned at whatever edge you've stood at.
