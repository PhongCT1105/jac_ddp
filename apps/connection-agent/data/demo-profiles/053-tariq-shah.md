The shop's been on the same corner since my father opened it in 1974, and the sawdust still goes down the way he taught me, though lately half my head is somewhere else — doing sums on what the restaurants two streets over throw in their bins each night. I took up the trade because it was expected of me, then stayed because I found I liked the precision: knowing where a knife wants to go, reading a carcass the way some people read a room. What pulled me sideways was watching good cuts I'd have sold gladly, and offal nobody had asked a chef to try, go straight into landfill.

Break my week down the way I'd break down a side of beef, and here's what's left on the block:

- I log what three local kitchens discard weekly, mostly so I have numbers when I argue with their managers instead of just opinions.
- I buy the trim and bones other suppliers won't bother with, then work out, cut by cut, what a decent kitchen could actually make of them.
- I still take real pleasure in the plain craft of the job, a clean seam, a well-hung side of beef, even in weeks the campaign eats every spare hour.
- I'm teaching myself food safety regulation properly, because a redistribution idea that can't survive a traceability question isn't worth pitching.

What I actually need right now is someone who can look at my traceability paperwork and tell me straight whether it would survive an inspector's second look — I can figure out the rest of this campaign myself.
