A photograph from 1962 has been pinned above my worktable for three months now — a woman in front of a fishing boat, wearing a yoke sweater whose pattern no surviving book records. I've spent evenings counting stitches in the grain of a scanned negative, arguing with myself about whether the decreases fall every fourth round or every fifth. This is roughly how I spend my days: taking garments that exist only as light and shadow on old paper and turning them back into something a person could actually knit from wool in hand.

I trained as a weaver, drifted into knitwear because shaping a garment around a body still surprises me after thirty-odd years, and now run a small workshop where envelopes arrive from strangers wanting their grandmother's sweater rebuilt.

A few questions I keep circling:

- Whether a pattern reconstructed straight from a photograph is more honest than one "improved" with modern shaping — my answer changes by the week.
- How much traditional handwork survives only because someone was willing to be tedious about it, and whether that's admirable or a warning sign.
- What a garment loses when it stops being made for one particular body and starts being sized off a chart.

I'll happily derail dinner arguing about why two-ply lopi drapes nothing like three-ply. I want to meet other people who work stubbornly with their hands, archivists who think about how objects hold information, and anyone who still cares whether a stitch count is right.
