The oldest mechanism on my workbench right now is a warded lock from a Hanseatic-era chest, pulled from a Tallinn attic by a client who thought it was scrap iron. I make my living opening doors people have locked themselves out of, but the hours that matter most to me are spent afterward, tracing how a mechanism traveled here and what it was trusted to guard. Twenty-some years of this trade has left me calloused and patient in a specific way.

What keeps me at the bench past closing is less the metal than the question behind it: a society tells you what it fears by what it bothers to lock. Grain stores got heavier hardware than libraries for most of history. Wedding chests got more elaborate locks than front doors. That asymmetry says something true that no chronicle records.

Catalogue enough historical mechanisms and the same handful of questions start resurfacing:

- Whether a repair should restore an object to working order or simply to honesty about its age - I don't think those are always the same goal.
- How much of "security" is actually about the ritual of turning a key, rather than the mechanism itself.
- What gets thrown away versus preserved, and whether craftsmanship or sentiment decides that more often.

I'm slow to trust and quick to notice when someone is only half listening, but a real thread - an inherited object, a story about what was once worth guarding - will keep me past closing time. Tell me: what do you keep locked, and what does that say about you?
