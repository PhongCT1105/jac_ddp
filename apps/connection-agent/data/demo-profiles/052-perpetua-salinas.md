A kindergarten teacher once asked me to get a four-year-old to "just pick one language" before he confused himself further. He wasn't confused. He was doing something harder than most adults attempt, and doing it well. That conversation, years ago in a San Antonio school, is a big part of why I still do this work: sorting out which sounds are hard because of a real delay, and which are hard because someone decided bilingualism was the problem.

Here's what doesn't fit in the box labeled "language interference," but holds up every time I test it against an actual kid:

- A language gap is not the same thing as a language disorder, and mixing up the two costs families years of unnecessary worry.
- Kids code-switch for reasons that make sense once you're listening for them, not because they've lost track of themselves.
- Progress often shows up first in the language nobody's testing, so a narrow assessment can miss a kid who's actually doing fine.
- Parents usually know more than the paperwork gives them credit for, and the best plans get built with them, not handed to them.

Outside the clinic I referee my nephews' fútbol matches badly and I'm relearning piano I quit at twelve out of spite. The argument I keep losing in rooms with no other bilingual person in them: two languages aren't a disorder, the rubric is what's broken. I want someone to hand me a sharper version of that argument than the one I've got.
