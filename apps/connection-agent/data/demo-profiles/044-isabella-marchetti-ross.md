Most of my week happens in a demountable classroom on Sydney's western edge, across a low table from kids whose files are thicker than their years at school. The real work isn't the standardized battery I'm required to run — it's giving a nine-year-old who's decided adults don't have time for him forty minutes of proof otherwise.

I still administer the assessments, because the system won't release a resource without a score attached, but I've stopped believing the numbers tell the whole story. A percentile can't capture a kid who translated for his parents at the bank the night before a reading test. I write the reports the department wants and smuggle the truth in around the edges.

Questions I keep carrying between sessions:

- Whether a diagnosis opens a door for a family, or just gives a school a reason to stop trying.
- How much of a "behavioural problem" is a kid doing the sanest thing available in an unreasonable situation.
- What an assessment built around resilience, rather than deficit, would look like.
- When to push for more support, and when it becomes one more appointment a family can't reach.

Outside work I run slowly along the river most mornings, mainly to think without a child or a form in front of me. I want to talk with people rebuilding intake systems from scratch, or anyone who's stayed curious inside a system while still arguing with it.
