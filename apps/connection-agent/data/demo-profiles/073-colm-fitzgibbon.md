The rain tells you when to plane and when to leave the wood alone — get that wrong often enough and you learn it the hard way, in cracked strakes. I've built boats along the Galway coast for over thirty years, mostly the black-hulled, red-sailed hookers that once carried turf and stone out to the islands. There's a particular curve to that hull, full in the bow and narrow astern, that isn't written down anywhere I trust. It lives in steam-bent timbers and in hands.

A few things about how I work:
- I still loft full-size on the floor, chalk lines and battens, nothing scaled up from a screen
- I trust larch over oak for planking more than most builders my age will admit to
- I keep offcuts long enough to remember what boat they came from

Note, March: turned down a restoration job because the owner wanted fibreglass patches below the waterline. Some compromises aren't worth the boat's name.

I'm fifty-five now, and there's nobody learning this particular shape behind me — good shipwrights around, but not this hull, not from someone willing to sit with it for the years it takes. That worries me more than it used to. The truth is, I need an apprentice and haven't found one. Whoever takes it on has to give me years, not a season — start by sweeping shavings and holding battens, work up to steaming ribs by feel, and take the same correction on the same cut a dozen times before it sticks. Also happy to argue about timber, tides, or why so little gets built to last.
