**A TASTING MENU, IN LIEU OF A BIOGRAPHY**
*Served in a kitchen off Rue de Flandre, Brussels, most evenings after six*

---

**AMUSE-BOUCHE — one spoonful, to start**

I'm forty-six, I've lived in Brussels nineteen years, and most of my working life goes into one stubborn question: where did this dish actually come from, and what did it give up to get here. Not the version printed on a menu with a little flag next to it — the tangled real answer, usually involving a port, a famine, a garrison, or a grandmother who never wrote anything down.

**FIRST COURSE — how the plate got set**

I trained as a historian, not a cook. My early research traced Antwerp's spice trade and the households that quietly ran on labor whose names never made the ledgers. In my thirties I got tired of writing about food without cooking it, so I started reconstructing dishes straight from the sources — a stew from a 1920s household inventory, a bread from a convent account book. Cooking the dish before I write about it keeps me honest about what the page leaves out.

**SECOND COURSE — the ingredients I keep returning to**

Migration, mostly — not the tidy "fusion" story, but the coercive routes food actually takes: what an administration extracted and imposed in return, what a diaspora kitchen preserved on purpose and lost by accident, what gets called "authentic" by people never asked to define it. Three years now I've been tracing one stew across four countries and a century and a half, trying to hold what different communities remember differently instead of picking a winner.

**THIRD COURSE — method, more than result**

Archive in the morning, market in the afternoon, stove in the evening. I read a source, buy what it asks for as closely as I can, and cook it exactly as written before I improvise. Most of what I learn lives in the gap between instruction and result — a pot of a different shape, a pepper no longer grown, a "good handful" that means something precise by the fortieth attempt.

**INTERMEZZO — what I'm still hungry for**

Oral history. Written sources are what I trained on, but the people who cooked most of these dishes rarely left documents — they left daughters, church cookbooks, memory. I'm learning to ask without leading the answer.

**DIGESTIF — who I'd like across the table**

No dish belongs to only one place, and that doesn't erase where it was made or by whom — I'd rather sit with that tension than pick a side. So: archivists, translators, agronomists tracing seed lineages, anyone restoring an old grain or fruit variety. Home cooks holding a family recipe with no written version behind it. People doing similar work with music, textiles, or language. Bring a dish's history you've never fully verified — that's usually where the good conversation starts.
