The wheel doesn't care what language you throw in. That was the first thing I relearned, two years ago, in a rented studio with borrowed tools and clay nothing like what I'd spent twenty years understanding with my hands. The old clay was short and forgiving; this one is plastic and stubborn and remembers everything you do to it. I threw eleven bowls the first week. I kept one.

I'm thirty-five, and I make pots for a living — simple enough, until you've had to build the life around it twice. I left my first studio with two suitcases and a box of glaze test tiles I refused to abandon, no equipment at all: no wheel, no kiln, none of the clay body I'd spent a decade calibrating recipes for. Rebuilding wasn't romantic. Mostly it was logistics — a kiln I could afford, a local clay that fires two cones hotter and warps if you're not watching it, the word for "reduction" turning out not to be the word I'd learned from a textbook, which earned me a patient, amused correction from an eighty-year-old potter who's since become something between a mentor and a rival. We disagree constantly about glaze chemistry and look forward to it every week.

The language is still a wall I walk into sideways most days. I can discuss flux ratios with real precision, learned flashcard by flashcard, before I could reliably order food without pointing. Fluent in cobalt oxide, helpless with small talk — better now, though I still rehearse phone calls out loud, alone, before I make them.

What I make is different too, not only because the materials forced it. The old work was thin-walled and symmetrical, a control I was proud of and now find cold in photographs. What comes off this wheel is heavier, less apologetic — a deliberate list to one side, a lip not quite even. I used to fire those as failures. Now I fire them as the piece.

I fire wood twice a year with a small, argumentative collective: three days with almost no sleep, meals cooked over the fire eating the kiln's fuel, the intimacy of watching a gauge with strangers at four in the morning, deciding whether to stoke again. Some of my closest ties here started as someone handing me a piece of pine and saying "your turn," before they knew I could hold a conversation past three words.

There's a second stool at the wheel, spattered with the same slip as mine, angled to catch the light from the north window. I built this studio around one pair of hands and left room for a second out of habit, the way you set out two coffee cups without deciding to. It's stayed empty longer than I meant it to. What I keep picturing isn't a type of person — it's someone finally in that seat long enough that the light moves before they stand up, hands gone still on a lump of clay they've forgotten they're kneading, more interested in whatever we're arguing about than in leaving.
