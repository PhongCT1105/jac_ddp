Tuesday's docket had eleven names on it and I'd properly prepared four. That ratio hasn't moved much in nine years, and it's as honest a starting point as any for describing what I do.

I'm a public defender in Toronto — bail hearings, disclosure fights, the low hum of a system that runs on paper, patience, and other people's worst weeks. A few things I've learned along the way:

- Most clients need someone who returns calls before they need a legal argument.
- The job rewards stubbornness more than brilliance.
- Burnout doesn't announce itself. It quietly narrows what you notice.

I keep a running list of procedural questions on my phone that would make decent footnotes if I ever had a free weekend for writing. Mostly I don't. What I actually want next is repair — sleep, a hobby that isn't reading transcripts, maybe a rowing machine again before I invent excuses not to.

Good conversation, for me: justice policy without slogans, people who rebuilt a working life mid-career, anyone who can explain court backlogs to a skeptical dinner table. I'm not fishing for case stories to trade. I'd rather talk about what keeps a person doing unglamorous, necessary work for the long stretch — and whether that's sustainable, or just what we tell ourselves at 11pm before the next filing.
