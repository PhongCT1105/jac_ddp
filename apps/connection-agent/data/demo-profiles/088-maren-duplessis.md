Before I trained as a teacher I trained as an interrupter. That was my first job out of university, badly paid and badly named: I sat in mainstream classrooms next to kids labelled disruptive and tried to catch what they were actually saying underneath the disruption. Usually it was something reasonable, in a language nobody in the room had learned. Eleven years in special needs education here in Cape Town, the last seven mostly with non-speaking students, turned that habit into a second sight I can't switch off. I read the set of a stranger's shoulders in a pharmacy queue the way other people read headlines.

The turning point wasn't dramatic. My third year in, I watched a colleague spend forty minutes trying to get a student to point at a laminated card, while the student had been telling us both, through breathing and the angle of his chair and where his attention kept sliding, that he wanted the room quieter. Nobody had asked the room. I started asking the room. My curriculum now has less to do with the speech-generating devices and eye-gaze boards I still use daily, and more to do with treating behaviour as information: the rhythm of a hand against a desk, how long a pause is held, the difference between a silence that means no and one that means I'm still arriving. None of it is mystical. It's attention, held until it stops looking like effort.

I'm not a patient person by temperament, which surprises people who assume the job selects for saints. I talk fast, interrupt my friends constantly, and hold loud opinions about municipal politics I'll defend without irony. The stillness I bring to a classroom is a discipline I built, not a personality I was issued, and some days it costs more than I let on. I decompress doing the opposite: singing alto in a choir that rehearses too loudly in an Observatory church hall, and slowly, incompetently learning to surf off Muizenberg.

I want conversation that treats difference in how a mind works as data, not a deficit to manage around. I'd like to know people building augmentative communication technology, since most of what's on the market was designed by people who've never used it under stress. I'd like to know interpreters, translators, choir conductors, anyone who has spent real time in the distance between intention and expression, and teachers who've stopped mistaking a rigid curriculum for rigor. I'm equally happy discussing tidal patterns or why one vowel sound in Afrikaans makes my chest ache. I don't do well with people who filter everything through inspiration; I do well with people who ask specific, uncomfortable questions and wait for the answer.
