## The Vat Keeps Its Own Time

Growing up in Hanoi, I never touched an indigo vat until I was twenty-two, on what was meant to be a two-day sketching trip north. I stayed a week. What held me was not the color but the waiting: a vat has to be fed and rested before it gives up the blue everyone recognizes. Six years later I still spend a third of my year in the highlands, learning from dyers, many of them Nùng and Dao women carrying skills their mothers passed down, how fermentation shifts with humidity, season, and whose hands do the stirring.

## Two Clocks That Refuse to Sync

My real job is negotiating between timelines that do not agree. A vat needs weeks; a fashion calendar wants four collections a year and rewards whoever cuts corners fastest. I have watched studios solve that mismatch by reducing indigo to a swatch code, and I refuse to. So I write contracts with longer lead times, price cloth for what the labor actually costs, and insist credit for a design goes to the household that dyed it, not only the brand selling it.

## Currently Stuck On

A textile that fades honestly, showing its age instead of hiding it, and how to explain that to buyers trained to expect uniformity. I want to compare notes with dyers from other natural traditions, with people who understand fair-trade supply chains from the finance side, and with anyone running a small textile business who has had to say no to a client to protect the people upstream.
