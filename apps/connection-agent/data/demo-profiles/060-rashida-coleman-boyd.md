Ask me what a good day at work looks like and I'll tell you: nothing happened. No line went sideways, no site ran out of forms, no volunteer called me in a panic at six in the morning. I coordinate polling logistics for a Milwaukee county office, which mostly means making sure a Tuesday in November is forgettable. People think the job is dramatic. It's closer to running a fleet of small, temporary restaurants that all have to open on time.

I'm 46, and I've been at this long enough to have opinions about folding tables, ramp placement, and the exact way a nervous first-time volunteer needs to be talked to at 5:45 a.m. before doors open. What keeps me in it is the choreography — dozens of sites, hundreds of people, one shared deadline nobody can move.

What makes it into my post-election debrief memo, year after year:

- Boring is a design goal, not an accident, and getting there takes more planning than anything flashy would.
- The best volunteers aren't the most confident ones on day one, they're the ones who ask what happens if something breaks.
- Every process I inherit was built by someone solving a real problem, even when the reason isn't written down.
- Training sticks better as a story than a checklist, so I've started collecting the stories.

Outside work I restore old radios badly and garden well. Nobody thanks you for the shift where nothing happened — the thank-yous only show up on the bad years, which tells you something about how this job gets noticed at all.
