I used to tell the story in one sentence: church basement in Dorchester, folding chairs, bad coffee, eleven years ago. Clean line, easy to say, and it left out the two years before that where things came apart slowly enough that I could keep telling myself it wasn't happening. My father's electrical shop closed the year I turned twenty-six, and I inherited his habit of needing something to do with my hands at midnight, minus the shop. I was good at the work — commercial wiring, mostly, some residential on the side — and for a long time I could point to that and say the rest didn't matter. It mattered. It cost me a marriage and about four years I don't remember cleanly, and I'm not filling those years in here. That part of the story belongs to the people who lived through it with me, not to a page like this.

What I do now is train peer recovery counselors — people using their own lived experience to support others who are early in it or still deciding. I run a lot of the practicum sessions, the ones where someone has to sit across from a person in a hard moment and say something useful instead of something rehearsed. My main job in those rooms is teaching people how much of their own story to spend and when. New counselors tend to either give away everything in the first ten minutes, like proof of membership, or clam up because they're scared of centering themselves. Neither works. I've spent a decade calibrating that dial for myself and I still get it wrong some weeks.

The part I'm still working out: I'm better at helping other people regulate than I am at doing it myself. I have a short fuse in traffic that eleven years of sobriety hasn't touched at all. I quit drinking, never quit smoking, and have made an uneasy peace with that hypocrisy. My son is sixteen now and mostly remembers the after-version of me, which is a relief and also its own strange grief — he doesn't know the person his mother married, and some days I think he should, and other days I think he's lucky he doesn't.

I read more theory than most people in this field admit to — motivational interviewing, sure, but also books on how people change their minds about anything, habit, identity, shame. I box twice a week at a gym that doesn't care what anyone's story is, which I find restful. I rebuild old lamps and radios on weekends, wiring being the one part of my old trade I never stopped loving even after I stopped needing it to pay rent.

I'm looking for people building training curricula in adjacent fields — nursing, teaching, chaplaincy, anywhere someone turns a hard personal history into a usable professional skill without losing themselves in the performance of it. I'd also like more friends who have nothing to do with recovery work, who'll talk to me about lamp rewiring or bad boxing form instead of asking, gently, how I'm doing.
