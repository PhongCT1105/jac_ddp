My hand knows a particular gray for the hour before dawn in an animated Seoul that doesn't exist — I've mixed it so often it arrives before I've thought about it. Eleven years in background departments now, mostly empty rooms and weather: rain on a window nobody watches, a stairwell lit for a conversation just off-frame, a field under a sky that says what the characters won't. My work is meant to disappear; noticing the background instead of the scene means I've failed, and most days that suits me. Some days it doesn't, and I catch myself in front of an empty canvas, wondering what a room built around me — not someone else's homecoming — looks like.

Questions I keep circling on the walk home:

- Whether a place can hold feeling without a person in it, or whether I'm just borrowing the audience's memory and calling it atmosphere.
- How much of my eye is training — years of being told which grays read as sadness — versus something that was mine before the job shaped it.
- What I'd paint if no character ever had to walk through it, a room that exists for its own weather, not as a stage.

What I keep picturing isn't a person so much as a table: late, the check settled, neither of us reaching for a coat, one lamp on, the rest of the room let go dark on purpose. Something unfinished between us — a sketch, an argument, a plan neither explains — in no hurry to resolve. That's the scene I want: no character required, just the light held a little longer than it needs to be.
