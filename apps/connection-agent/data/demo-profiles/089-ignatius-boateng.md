Thirty-eight years I stood at the same desk at the Kumasi office, stamping declarations, weighing what people said against what their trucks actually carried, and learning more about human nature from a manifest sheet than most people learn from a lifetime of conversation. You would be surprised what a person's paperwork tells you before they open their mouth. I retired four years ago, and the habit that never left me is this: I cannot accept a parcel, even from my own children, without running my thumb along the seams of the box before I open it, checking for a false bottom that of course is never there. There is nothing now to inspect except my own garden and, lately, a set of beehives that are testing my patience in ways no smuggler ever managed.

I keep four hives behind the house, and I will tell you plainly that I am not good at it. My friend Kwabena, who has kept bees since he was a boy, laughs at me twice a week when he stops by to see what I have done wrong this time. I have been stung in places I will not describe. I lost an entire hive last harmattan season because I would not listen when he told me to move them out of the wind. But there is something about standing near a box of ten thousand creatures who owe you nothing and cooperate anyway that I did not get from four decades of enforcing regulations. The bees do not care about my career. They only care whether I have understood the weather, and mostly I have not, and I keep trying anyway.

The letters started before the bees, actually. My eldest daughter's children live in Toronto now, and my second son's family is in Accra, close enough to visit but far enough that a boy of nine forgets his grandfather's voice if he does not hear it often. So I write to them. Not short notes — proper letters, sometimes six or seven pages, about things that happened at the border post decades ago, about my own father who sold kente cloth in the market and could tell the difference between real dye and cheap imitation from ten feet away, about what Kumasi smelled like before the roads were paved. In the last one I sent to the boy in Toronto, I let myself say something I have not managed to say out loud to anyone here: "What your grandfather misses, now that the desk is empty, is not the work itself — it is being interrupted mid-sentence by someone whose read on a person was earned by forty years of watching, not handed to them by a manual. I miss being surprised by people who worked out how the world runs the hard way, same as I did." My daughter tells me the grandchildren read them aloud to each other, which I did not expect and which undid me a little the first time I heard it.

Customs work teaches you to be suspicious of a story that is too smooth, and I carried that instinct home with me longer than was fair to my own family. I was hard on my children in ways I have had to apologize for as an old man, which is a strange thing to do — to sit your grown son down and tell him you were wrong about how you raised him. He forgave me faster than I forgave myself. That is probably the truest thing I know about being a father: the apology matters less for its timing than for whether it finally comes.

I do not miss the uniform, but I miss certain colleagues, the ones who understood that the job was really about judgment, not rules — knowing when a nervous trader was hiding contraband and when he was just a man terrified of losing a shipment his family's whole month depended on. I am, without much shame at seventy, still hoping Kwabena's patience with me outlasts my own patience with the bees, and I would welcome anyone whose grandchildren are growing up far away and who has found a way to stay real to children who are growing up somewhere else entirely.
