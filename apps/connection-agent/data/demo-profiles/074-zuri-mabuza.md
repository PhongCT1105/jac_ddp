Tuesday rehearsal note: the hall smells of floor wax and the diesel drifting up from the taxi rank next door — which is more or less the set design done for me already.

I make dance out of the parts of a day nobody thinks to look at twice: the queue at the rank, the fourteen minutes on a minibus when everyone studiously ignores everyone else, the specific boredom of waiting that isn't boredom at all because your body is still braced for the next thing to happen. Durban traffic has taught me more about tempo than any teacher managed to.

Notes I give the dancers before we run this section again:
- rhythm that comes from infrastructure rather than music
- how a crowd of strangers becomes briefly one shape, then scatters
- what a borrowed, unglamorous room forces you to invent when you can't afford better

I trained formally and then spent a few years quietly un-training myself, working instead with commuters, market traders, a security guard who has the most economical shrug I've ever seen. None of them made their movement for a stage, which is exactly why I wanted it.

What I'm short on right now is sound — someone who treats ambient noise as composition rather than backdrop. I'd also like to talk to transit planners, or anyone who has spent real time thinking about queues, thresholds, and why waiting rooms feel the same in every city.
